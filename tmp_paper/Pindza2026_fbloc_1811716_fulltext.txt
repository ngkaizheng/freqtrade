Microstructure alpha: hierarchical 
learning and cross-asset transfer 
in cryptocurrency markets
Edson Pindza*
Department of Decision Sciences, College of Economics and Management Sciences, University of South 
Africa, Pretoria, South Africa
Introduction: We test whether classical market microstructure features can 
predict short-term cryptocurrency returns once data leakage and trading costs 
are properly accounted for.
Methods: Using over three million minute-level observations from six major 
cryptocurrencies on Binance spot and perpetual futures markets, we evaluate 
nine microstructure measures through a pipeline that includes hierarchical 
modelling, stability selection, gradient boosting with SHAP, meta-learning, and 
purged walk-forward cross-validation benchmarked against naive forecasters.
Results:All features are stably selected at minute frequency, with range-based 
spread proxies and realised volatility the most robust. However, gradient-boosted 
models overfit severely under proper leakage controls, and no strategy survives 
realistic exchange fees. Models trained on one cryptocurrency do not transfer to 
others, although they transfer well between the spot and futures venues of the 
same asset.
Discussion:The results show that microstructure signals carry genuine but weak 
information content that is useful for understanding market quality but not 
exploitable at standard retail fee levels.

1 Introduction
The rapid evolution of cryptocurrency markets has created a fertile environment for 
studying market microstructure phenomena at unprecedented granularity. Unlike 
traditional equity markets with their fragmented venue structures and complex order 
routing protocols, cryptocurrency exchanges provide continuous trading with publicly 
accessible order book and trade data, enabling researchers to examine the formation of 
prices and the dynamics of liquidity provision with exceptional clarity (Makarov and Schoar, 
2020; Biais et al., 2023).
Market microstructure theory, as pioneered by Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom 
(1985), provides a rich theoretical foundation for understanding how private information is 
incorporated into prices through the trading process. The fundamental insight that price 
impact reflects the adverse selection costs faced by liquidity providers has motivated an 
extensive literature on measuring and predicting the informed trading component of order 
flow (Easley et al., 1996; Hasbrouck, 2021). In the cryptocurrency setting, these dynamics are 
potentially magnified by the prevalence of algorithmic trading, the global and continuous 
nature of markets, and the absence of traditional institutional gatekeepers (Cong 
et al., 2022).

We use these classical models as reduced-form benchmarks 
rather than as literal descriptions of a human specialist market. On 
centralized cryptocurrency exchanges such as Binance, liquidity is 
supplied predominantly by algorithmic limit-order strategies, but 
the economic primitives remain closely related to Kyle and 
Glosten–Milgrom: aggressive order flow moves prices when it 
consumes depth, and passive liquidity suppliers widen or 
withdraw quotes when order flow is more toxic. For automated- 
market-maker decentralized exchanges the mechanism differs 
because prices are set by a liquidity-pool rule rather than by a 
central limit order book; nevertheless, recent DeFi microstructure 
evidence shows that adverse selection, price impact, liquidity 
concentration, and informed liquidity provision remain first- 
order objects of study (Klein et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024). Our 
empirical setting is therefore best interpreted as a leakage-controlled 
test of classical microstructure signals in algorithmic, fragmented 
crypto limit-order-book markets, with DEX/AMM markets treated 
as a related but structurally distinct frontier.
This paper contributes to the nascent literature on 
cryptocurrency market microstructure by developing a 
comprehensive empirical framework that integrates multiple 
streams of microstructure theory into a unified predictive model. 
We construct features motivated by classical microstructure 
measures including the bid-ask spread, which reflects the costs of 
providing immediacy, Kyle’s lambda measuring price impact per 
unit of order flow, the Amihud illiquidity ratio capturing the price 
response to trading volume, the volume-synchronized probability of 
informed trading providing a flow-based toxicity measure, and 
order flow imbalance indicating the directional pressure from 
aggressive traders.
Our methodological approach combines several complementary 
analytical tools. First, we employ hierarchical linear models with 
mixed effects to capture both the universal relationships between 
microstructure features and returns that hold across all assets, as well 
as asset-specific deviations that reflect the unique trading 
characteristics of individual cryptocurrencies. Second, we 
implement stability selection through randomized Lasso to 
identify which microstructure features provide robust predictive 
information that persists across different subsamples and 
regularization strengths. Third, we utilize gradient-boosted 
decision trees with Shapley additive explanations to capture 
potentially nonlinear relationships and interactions among 
features while maintaining interpretability regarding each 
feature’s marginal contribution to predictions.
A central innovation of our analysis is the systematic 
investigation of cross-asset transfer learning in the microstructure 
context. We train predictive models on one cryptocurrency and 
evaluate their out-of-sample performance on other assets, both 
within and across market venues. This transfer learning 
perspective addresses a fundamental question for market 
participants: to what extent do the microstructure dynamics 
learned from one market generalize to other related markets? 
The answer has practical implications for trading strategy 
development, risk management, and the understanding of market 
integration.
Our empirical analysis draws on 3,417,972 minute-level 
observations spanning August 2025 to February 2026, covering 
six major cryptocurrencies—Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, 
Avalanche, Chainlink, and Polkadot—across both spot and 
perpetual futures markets on the Binance exchange 
(approximately 285,000 bars per asset–venue pair). The minute- 
level granularity is critical for our analysis: it provides the statistical 
power necessary to resolve weak microstructure signals that are 
smoothed away at hourly or daily frequencies, while the 6.5-month 
window captures a representative cross-section of market conditions 
during the 2025–2026 expansion phase. The target variable is the 5- 
min forward log return, which strikes a balance between the noise of 
tick-level returns and the signal attenuation of longer horizons.
The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2
reviews the relevant theoretical and empirical literature on market 
microstructure and machine learning in financial markets. Section 3
describes our microstructure feature construction methodology. 
Section 4 presents the hierarchical modeling framework and 
transfer learning approach. Section 5 reports our empirical 
results. Section 6 discusses implications and limitations, and 
Section 7 concludes with directions for future research.
2 Literature review
The theoretical foundations of our analysis rest on the seminal 
contributions to market microstructure theory developed over the 
past 4 decades. Kyle (1985) introduced the continuous-time model 
of strategic trading by an informed trader facing competitive market 
makers, deriving the equilibrium price impact function and 
establishing that prices follow a martingale with respect to public 
information. The Kyle lambda parameter, measuring price impact 
per unit of order flow, has become a cornerstone measure of market 
quality and information asymmetry (Hasbrouck, 2009).
Glosten and Milgrom (1985)developed the foundational model 
of bid-ask spreads as compensation for adverse selection risk, 
demonstrating that the spread must be at least large enough to 
cover the expected losses from trading with better-informed 
counterparties. This framework was extended by Glosten (1994)
to analyze the implications for market structure and by Huang and 
Stoll (1997) to decompose spreads into their constituent parts. The 
adverse selection component of spreads has been shown to contain 
predictive information for future returns (Brennan et al., 1998).
The relevance of these models to cryptocurrency markets 
requires some qualification. Kyle’s original model assumes a 
strategic informed trader and competitive market makers, while 
Glosten–Milgrom is framed around sequential trade with 
heterogeneously informed traders. Modern crypto trading 
replaces the human specialist with algorithmic quoting, cross- 
exchange arbitrage, and latency-sensitive inventory control. This 
does not invalidate the models, but it changes their interpretation: 
Kyle’s λ is a reduced-form price-impact elasticity, and the bid-ask 
spread is a reduced-form compensation for inventory, adverse- 
selection, latency, and operational risks. This interpretation is 
consistent with recent evidence that crypto order flow and limit- 
order activity contribute materially to price discovery, while high- 
frequency integration across venues remains imperfect (Alexander 
et al., 2021). It also aligns with evidence from Binance fee changes 
showing that market-maker incentives can widen spreads and 
reduce displayed depth even when explicit trading fees fall 
(Galati, 2024).

The measurement of informed trading activity has received 
substantial attention in the empirical microstructure literature. 
Easley et al. (1996) introduced the probability of informed 
trading (PIN) model, which uses the sequential arrival of buy 
and sell orders to infer the probability that a given trade is 
information-motivated. Easley et al. (2012) developed the 
volume-synchronized probability of informed trading (VPIN), 
which provides a higher-frequency measure better suited to 
modern electronic markets. The VPIN metric has been shown to 
spike prior to extreme price movements and market stress events 
(Abad and Yagüe, 2012).
A rapidly growing recent literature now studies whether these 
same concepts survive in crypto-specific market structures. Easley 
et al. (2024) show that liquidity and price-discovery measures help 
explain and predict the dynamics of major cryptocurrencies, with 
own-market and cross-market effects for Roll measures and VPIN. 
Pinto (2025)documents high-frequency microstructure regularities 
in Binance Bitcoin and Ethereum perpetual futures over 2020–2024, 
emphasizing the importance of trade size and intraday aggregation. 
These studies support the use of high-frequency microstructure 
variables in cryptocurrency markets, while also showing why leakage 
controls and careful horizon choice are essential.
The application of machine learning methods to financial 
prediction has grown rapidly in recent years. Gu et al. (2020)
provided a comprehensive comparison of machine learning 
approaches for equity return prediction, finding that neural 
networks and tree-based methods substantially outperform linear 
models. Chen et al. (2023)extended this analysis to cryptocurrency 
markets, demonstrating the potential for deep learning to capture 
complex patterns in high-frequency data. The use of gradient- 
boosted trees with SHAP-based interpretation has been 
advocated by Lundberg and Lee (2017) as providing both 
predictive accuracy and model interpretability.
Recent cryptocurrency return-prediction work also points to the 
promise and risk of flexible models. Filippou et al. (Filippou et al., 
2024) find that machine-learning methods improve daily 
cryptocurrency return forecasts and that momentum, size, value, 
and online-activity variables are informative. Our study differs in 
two respects: we work at the 5-min horizon using explicitly 
microstructural predictors, and we evaluate models under purged 
walk-forward validation and explicit trading costs. This distinction 
matters because high-frequency crypto data make it easy for 
nonlinear models to fit transient market states that vanish once 
temporal leakage and turnover costs are controlled.
Transfer learning in financial applications has been explored by 
Ye et al. (2020)in the context of portfolio optimization and by Han 
et al. (2020)for cross-market return prediction. The ability to transfer 
learned representations across related prediction tasks has important 
implications for developing robust trading strategies and 
understanding the common factors that drive asset returns.
Cryptocurrency market microstructure has been studied by 
Makarov and Schoar (2020) who documented substantial price 
discrepancies across exchanges, by Griffin and Shams (Griffin 
and Shams, 2020) who examined potential market manipulation, 
and by Cong et al. (2021)who developed theoretical models of token 
market dynamics. The perpetual futures market has been analyzed 
by Alexander and Dakos (2020) who examined the basis and roll 
returns. Recent decentralized-exchange work further shows that 
market quality in crypto cannot be understood only through 
centralized order books. Klein et al. (2023) document informed 
liquidity provision on decentralized exchanges, while Zhu et al. 
(2024) show that liquidity concentration, gas prices, markouts, and 
private market-maker inventory shape Uniswap market depth. 
These papers highlight the frontier raised by the editor: 
algorithmic and decentralized mechanisms alter the institutional 
details of price formation, but they do not eliminate the central 
microstructure questions of adverse selection, liquidity supply, price 
impact, and transferability across venues.
Our contribution is positioned against this current literature as 
follows. We do not claim that Kyle and Glosten–Milgrom are 
complete structural models of decentralized finance. Instead, we 
use their core empirical implications to construct interpretable 
features for algorithmic centralized crypto markets, then test 
whether those features survive modern validation standards. 
Relative to the recent crypto microstructure literature, the paper 
contributes a unified leakage-controlled comparison of spread, 
price-impact, toxicity, order-flow, momentum, and transfer- 
learning signals across six assets and two Binance venues, and it 
links statistical predictability to economic feasibility under explicit 
exchange fees.

4.1 Hierarchical linear model

hierarchical linear model with mixed effects. Let yit denote the 5-

strength across assets while accommodating the reality that 
different cryptocurrencies may have different baseline return

4.7 Leakage control and purged cross- 
validation
To rule out look-ahead leakage in feature construction we 
align every feature xit strictly to information available at the end 
of minute t, and target returns yit � log(Ci,t+5/Ci,t) using future 
closes only. Features constructed from rolling windows (VPIN 
over 50 bars, Kyle’s λ/Amihud/OFI over 30 bars, realised 
volatility over 60 bars, momentum over 5/30/60 bars) are 
therefore right-aligned and never incorporate the forecast 
interval. We further guard against leakage introduced by 
autocorrelation in returns by adopting the purged, 
embargoed k-fold cross-validation scheme of López de Prado 
(2018): each fold’s test block is preceded and followed by a 
purge window of 5 min (equal to the target horizon) and an 
additional embargo of 60 min, so that no training observation 
shares information with any test observation. All 
hyperparameter tuning, stability selection, and model 
comparison statistics reported in the next section use this 
purged walk-forward scheme with k� 5.

5 Empirical results
5.1 Data summary
Our analysis uses 3,417,972 minute-level Binance bars covering

5.8 Subsample and regime robustness
The purged out-of-sample R2 of a LightGBM model varies 
markedly across market regimes. In the low-volatility regime, 
R2 ≈ 0%; in up-market conditions, R2�+0.4%; but in the high-

conditions, R2� −39%. The catastrophic high-volatility and down- 
market performance confirms that LightGBM’s tree-based structure 
latches onto tail events in the training set that do not generalise out 
of sample. The regime analysis underscores that any apparent 
predictive content is concentrated in calm, trending markets and 
vanishes entirely during the stress episodes that matter most for risk 
management.

6 Discussion
The empirical findings presented in this paper have several 
important implications for our understanding of cryptocurrency 
market microstructure and the practical application of these insights 
to trading strategy development. In this section, we discuss the 
theoretical significance of our results, their practical implications, 
and the limitations of our analysis.
6.1 Theoretical implications
Our analysis provides new evidence on the information content 
of various microstructure measures in the cryptocurrency setting. 
The finding that the Corwin–Schultz spread proxy is the most 
statistically significant predictor of 5-min returns—while the 
Amihud ratio and Kyle’s λ are not individually significant in the 
hierarchical model—supports a view in which range-based spread 
measures capture the aggregate cost of immediacy more reliably 
than volume-based estimators at high frequency. In the 
cryptocurrency context, this may reflect the fact that minute-level 
volume is subject to wash-trading noise, whereas the high-low range 
is mechanically constrained by actual executions.
The relatively weak predictive power of the VPIN proxy, despite 
its theoretical foundation in the informed trading literature, suggests 
that the dynamics of information incorporation may differ in 
cryptocurrency markets compared to traditional equity markets. 
One possible explanation is that the global, continuous nature of 
cryptocurrency trading reduces the information asymmetries that 
the VPIN measure is designed to capture. Alternatively, the 
prevalence of algorithmic trading and high-frequency market 
making may have already arbitraged away much of the predictive 
content in order flow imbalances.
The dominance of momentum features in our SHAP analysis is 
consistent with the extensive literature documenting short-term 
return continuation in financial markets. However, the strength 
of this pattern in our cryptocurrency sample suggests that 
momentum effects may be particularly pronounced in these 
markets, possibly due to their relatively recent emergence and the 
predominance of retail traders who may exhibit stronger 
behavioral biases.
6.2 Comparison with traditional markets
Our findings allow for comparison with the existing literature on 
equity market microstructure. The magnitude of our best predictive 
improvement (ΔR2
OOS �+1.23% for OLS on microstructure 
features) is modest relative to the results reported in Gu et al. 
(2020)for U.S. equities, though direct comparison is complicated by 
differences in prediction horizons (5 minutes vs. monthly) and 
feature sets. The finding that transfer learning performs poorly 
across cryptocurrencies echoes results from the equity literature 
showing that factor models estimated on one subset of stocks often 
fail to generalize to other subsets.
The relationship between spot and futures markets that we 
document differs somewhat from the traditional equity literature. 
In equity markets, futures typically lead spot prices due to the 
concentration of informed trading in derivatives markets. Our 
finding of symmetric information transfer between spot and 
futures cryptocurrency markets suggests a more integrated price 
discovery process, potentially reflecting the unique characteristics of 
cryptocurrency trading including 24/7 operation and the absence of 
traditional institutional barriers.
6.3 Practical implications for trading
The results of our analysis have several practical implications for 
market participants. First, although all twelve features are stably 
selected at minute frequency, the catastrophic overfitting of 
LightGBM demonstrates that flexible nonlinear models are not 
the appropriate vehicle for exploiting weak microstructure signals 
in this setting. A parsimonious linear model on the microstructure 
features delivers the only marginal improvement over the random 
walk, and even that improvement is not statistically significant at 
conventional levels.
Second, the finding of limited cross-asset transfer suggests that 
trading models should be trained and calibrated separately for each 
cryptocurrency. While this increases the data requirements and 
computational burden of strategy development, it also suggests 
that the edge from microstructure analysis is unlikely to be 
quickly arbitraged away as different market participants may 
focus on different assets.

Third, the stronger transfer between spot and futures markets

trading strategies. Market makers providing liquidity in both venues 
could potentially use signals from one market to inform their 
quoting in the other, and arbitrageurs could exploit divergences 
in microstructure conditions across venues.
6.4 Implications for market quality
Our analysis also has implications for understanding market 
quality in cryptocurrency markets. The substantial variation in 
microstructure measures across assets and time suggests that 
liquidity conditions are far from uniform. Smaller 
cryptocurrencies exhibit wider spreads and higher price impact, 
indicating potential barriers to trading for these assets.
The finding that futures markets exhibit tighter spreads and 
lower price impact than spot markets suggests that the introduction 
of derivatives has improved price discovery and market quality for 
the underlying cryptocurrencies. This finding supports the 
theoretical prediction that derivatives markets provide a venue 
for informed trading that enhances the information content 
of prices.
6.5 Limitations
Several limitations of our analysis should be acknowledged. 
First, although our minute-level data provide substantially finer 
granularity than hourly studies, the Corwin–Schultz spread proxy 
remains a noisy surrogate for actual quoted spreads, and the order- 
flow imbalance measures constructed from taker-buy volume 
cannot separate different types of informed trading (e.g., news- 
based vs. inventory-based). Extending the analysis to tick data with 
full order-book snapshots would provide a more precise picture of 
microstructure dynamics. Second, the sample window of 
approximately 6.5 months (August 2025 – February 2026) 
captures only the 2025–2026 expansion phase; the findings may 
not generalise to bear markets or to the high-volatility crash episodes 
of 2020–2022.
Third, our analysis is limited to a single exchange (Binance), the 
largest by volume. Trading patterns, fee structures, and market- 
maker populations may differ across other exchanges (e.g., 
Coinbase, OKX, Bybit), and our findings may not generalise to a 
multi-exchange setting. In particular, cross-exchange arbitrage 
dynamics are absent from our framework.
Fourth, the economic evaluation (Section 5.7) accounts for 
Binance maker/taker fees and half-spread slippage but abstracts 
from queue-position dynamics, funding-rate costs on perpetual 
futures, borrow costs for short positions, and latency to the 
matching engine. The net Sharpe figures we report should 
therefore be read as an upper bound on achievable performance 
for a non-co-located retail-fee participant.
Fifth, the LightGBM overfitting documented in Section 5.6
— which only becomes visible under the purged walk-forward 
design—is a methodological warning that applies to any tree- 
based model applied to return prediction. While we have shown 
that a parsimonious OLS model marginally outperforms the random 
walk, the optimal model complexity for this task remains an 
open question.
Sixth, our MAML meta-learner is implemented as a linear base 
learner with a single inner-loop gradient step; this is far simpler than 
the neural-network architectures used in the image-classification 
meta-learning literature and may under-represent the potential of 
gradient-based adaptation. Exploring deeper architectures with 
second-order MAML variants is left for future work.

6.7 Robustness considerations
To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted several 
supplementary analyses. First, we re-estimated our models using 
alternative feature definitions, including different rolling window 
lengths and normalization methods. The qualitative findings 
remained similar, though the magnitude of predictive power 
varied across specifications.
Second, we examined the sensitivity of the cross-asset transfer 
results to the choice of training period. Using rolling windows of 
different lengths to train the source model, we found that longer 
training periods generally improved transfer performance, 
suggesting that the microstructure patterns we identify are 
relatively stable over time.
Third, we investigated whether the predictive relationships 
we identify vary across time of day or day of week. Preliminary 
results suggest some variation in predictability across trading 
sessions, with Asian trading hours showing somewhat different 
patterns than North American hours. This finding is consistent 
with the global nature of cryptocurrency trading and the 
different trader populations that may be active at 
different times.
7 Conclusion
This paper examined whether classical market microstructure 
features can reliably predict short-term cryptocurrency returns once 
data leakage and trading costs are properly controlled. Using over 
three million minute-level observations from six major 
cryptocurrencies on Binance spot and perpetual futures markets, 
we evaluated nine microstructure measures and three momentum 
controls through a rigorous pipeline that includes hierarchical 
modelling, stability selection, gradient boosting, meta-learning, 
and purged walk-forward cross-validation.
Our results can be summarised in four main findings. First, at 
the minute frequency and over a 6-month window, all twelve 
features pass the formal stability-selection threshold, with range- 
based spread proxies, realised volatility, and short-horizon 
momentum emerging as the most robust predictors. Second, a 
simple linear model on these features delivers a small 
improvement over the random walk under strict leakage controls, 
whereas a flexible gradient-boosted model overfits severely and 
performs worse than chance—a result that highlights the 
importance of proper temporal separation in financial machine- 
learning studies. Third, no trading strategy based on these 5-min 
forecasts survives standard Binance exchange fees and slippage, 
because the high rebalancing frequency generates extreme 
turnover that overwhelms the modest statistical edge. Fourth, 
microstructure models trained on one cryptocurrency do not 
generalise to other cryptocurrencies, although they transfer 
meaningfully between the spot and futures venues of the same 
underlying asset.
These findings have two broad implications. For researchers, the 
results demonstrate that leakage-controlled evaluation can overturn 
conclusions drawn from naive train-test splits and that the apparent 
success of complex machine-learning models in predicting 
cryptocurrency returns may largely reflect temporal 
contamination rather than genuine forecasting ability. For 
practitioners, the results confirm that microstructure signals at 
this frequency are too weak to overcome retail-level trading costs, 
though they remain useful as diagnostic indicators of market quality 
and liquidity conditions.
Several directions for future work emerge naturally from this 
study. Extending the sample to cover multiple market cycles 
would test whether the stability of microstructure features 
persists across bull, bear, and range-bound regimes. 
Incorporating alternative data sources such as on-chain 
transaction metrics, social media sentiment, and cross- 
exchange order flow could strengthen the predictive signal. 
Exploring deeper meta-learning architectures beyond the linear 
base learner used here may unlock the potential of gradient-based 
adaptation for cross-asset transfer. Repeating the analysis across 
multiple exchanges would clarify whether the findings are specific 
to Binance or reflect broader market properties. Finally, the 
purged cross-validation framework we employ could serve as a 
methodological standard for the wider cryptocurrency machine- 
learning literature, where temporal leakage remains a pervasive 
and underappreciated concern.

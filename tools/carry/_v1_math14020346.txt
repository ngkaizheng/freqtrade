Abstract
Perpetual futures account for approximately 93% of cryptocurrency futures trading volume,
yet funding rate dynamics across fragmented markets remain understudied. We construct
a high-frequency panel dataset comprising 35.7 million one-minute observations across
26 cryptocurrency exchanges (11 centralized, 15 decentralized) spanning 749 symbols over
eight consecutive days. Using time-series econometrics, correlation analysis, and Granger
causality tests, we characterize funding rate dynamics, market integration, and information
flow. We find evidence of a two-tiered market structure: centralized exchanges (CEX)
dominate price discovery with 61% higher integration than decentralized exchanges (DEX),
and all significant information flow runs CEX-to-DEX with zero reverse causality. While
17% of observations exhibit economically significant arbitrage spreads (≥20 basis points),
only 40% of top opportunities generate positive returns after transaction costs and spread
reversals. Delta-neutral portfolio simulations reveal that successful arbitrage requires both
high spreads and sufficient duration before inevitable reversals, with forced exits occurring
in 95% of opportunities. The findings show that cryptocurrency derivatives markets exhibit
a persistent two-tiered structure in which centralized platforms dominate price discov-
ery while transaction costs and spread reversal risks prevent arbitrage from eliminating
large mispricings between platforms, resolving the apparent paradox of substantial price
fragmentation coexisting with market efficiency.
Keywords: cryptocurrency risk management; statistical arbitrage; funding rate modeling;
portfolio optimization; quantitative finance

1. Introduction
Perpetual futures have become the dominant derivative instrument in cryptocurrency
markets, accounting for approximately 93% of cryptocurrency futures trading volume
and over $90 trillion in cumulative trading volume [1]. Unlike traditional contracts with
fixed expiration dates, these instruments remain open indefinitely; they employ what
practitioners call a funding rate mechanism (periodic payments between long and short
positions) to anchor the perpetual price to the underlying spot price. Without natural
convergence through expiration, this funding rate serves as the primary tool for maintaining
price parity, though how well it actually works varies considerably across different market

Trading activity in cryptocurrency perpetual futures markets is highly fragmented.
Dozens of exchanges differ fundamentally in organizational structure and market design.
Centralized exchanges (CEX), such as Binance and OKX, operate traditional order book
mechanisms with centralized custody; decentralized exchanges (DEX), including plat-
forms like HyperLiquid and Drift, employ on-chain settlement with permissionless access.
These structural differences affect liquidity provision, price discovery, and transaction
costs. Yet remarkably little is known about how funding rates compare across exchange
types or whether persistent arbitrage opportunities exist between them. In theory, arbi-
trage pressures should eliminate such discrepancies, but whether this holds in practice
remains unclear.
Three fundamental questions about funding rate dynamics in fragmented cryptocur-
rency markets drive this paper. First, what are the time-series properties of funding
rate spreads across exchanges? Do they exhibit mean reversion, volatility clustering,
and predictable persistence patterns? Second, what are the risk-adjusted returns of delta-
neutral arbitrage portfolios that exploit funding rate differentials; how do spread con-
vergence patterns affect optimal holding periods? Third, which exchanges lead funding
rate price discovery, and how does market integration differ between centralized and
decentralized platforms?
To address these questions, we construct a high-frequency panel dataset comprising
one-minute granularity funding rate observations across 26 cryptocurrency exchanges
(11 centralized and 15 decentralized) over eight consecutive days. This yields 35.7 million
observations spanning 749 cryptocurrency symbols. The findings appear to make three
contributions, though their novelty will depend on how the literature evolves. First, we
build a statistical framework characterizing funding rate dynamics through time-series
econometrics and transaction cost accounting. This framework provides evidence consistent
with existing theoretical work on perpetual futures pricing by documenting how real-
world market frictions create exploitable yet risky arbitrage opportunities [2,3]. Second,
methodologically, we introduce a normalization procedure for comparing funding rates
across heterogeneous platforms and demonstrate how to construct risk-managed multi-
venue arbitrage portfolios. Third, from a practical standpoint, our results might provide
guidance for traders implementing funding rate arbitrage strategies (optimal exchange
selection, position sizing, and risk management protocols).
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related liter-
ature on perpetual futures pricing, cryptocurrency market microstructure, and portfolio
optimization. Section 3 describes our data sources, normalization methodology, and econo-
metric approach. Section 4 presents our main empirical findings on funding rate dynamics,
arbitrage opportunities, and information flow across exchanges. Section 5 discusses the
economic mechanisms underlying our findings. Final conclusions are drawn in Section 6.
2. Literature Review
The theoretical foundation for perpetual futures originates with [4], who proposed
perpetual claims on economic indicators with funding rates dependent on observable cash
flows. Recent work has adapted this framework to cryptocurrency markets. Ref. [2] derive
explicit no-arbitrage prices for linear, inverse, and quanto perpetual futures. This provides
much of the theoretical foundation for understanding perpetual contract valuation. Ref. [3]
extend this analysis by characterizing no-arbitrage prices in frictionless markets and estab-
lishing pricing bounds when trading costs are present. While these studies establish the
theoretical properties of perpetual futures pricing, they largely abstract from institutional

heterogeneity across trading venues. This paper complements the literature by examining
how funding rates differ across exchange types and testing whether real-world frictions
generate persistent deviations from theoretical parity. Whether such deviations are more
transient than the literature suggests remains to be seen.
A separate strand of literature examines cross-sectional patterns in cryptocurrency
returns and market structure. Ref. [5] identify three common factors (cryptocurrency
market, size, momentum) that appear to capture expected returns in the cross-section of
cryptocurrencies, analogous to traditional asset pricing factors. Recent work on decen-
tralized finance (DeFi) highlights structural differences between CEX and DEX platforms.
DEX platforms typically employ automated market maker (AMM) mechanisms rather
than traditional order books, potentially affecting price discovery and arbitrage efficiency.
The magnitude of these effects remains somewhat unclear. Ref. [6] analyze arbitrage dy-
namics on decentralized exchanges, demonstrating how AMM design affects arbitrage
profitability and optimal trading strategies. Empirical studies document what seems to be
substantial arbitrage activity between CEX and DEX platforms. Ref. [7] find that searchers
extracted approximately $233.8 million from over 7.2 million arbitrages on Ethereum alone
between August 2023 and March 2025. These studies focus on spot price arbitrage rather
than funding rate arbitrage; systematic comparisons of funding rates across exchange types
have not yet been conducted.
Volatility and risk dynamics in cryptocurrency markets. Understanding volatility
is essential for risk management in cryptocurrency derivatives trading. Ref. [8] demon-
strates that GARCH models appear to capture the time-varying volatility in Bitcoin returns
effectively, with asymmetric specifications providing superior fit. Ref. [9] document that
cryptocurrency volatility exhibits distinct clustering patterns and responds asymmetrically
to positive and negative shocks, suggesting the need for sophisticated volatility modeling
for accurate risk assessment. Ref. [10] show that long-volatility strategies in cryptocurrency
markets can provide substantial risk-adjusted returns. This highlights the importance of
volatility dynamics for portfolio construction.
Portfolio optimization and risk management. The application of modern portfolio
theory to cryptocurrency markets has gained increasing attention. Ref. [11] apply mean-
variance optimization to cryptocurrency portfolios, finding that optimal portfolios may
significantly outperform naive diversification strategies. The stability of these results re-
mains an open question, however. Ref. [12] demonstrate that cryptocurrency-enhanced
portfolios appear to improve risk-adjusted returns for traditional investors; the magnitude
of benefits depends heavily on rebalancing frequency and transaction costs. Ref. [13] exam-
ine the stability of Sharpe ratios in cryptocurrency markets, documenting substantial time
variation that challenges static portfolio allocation rules. Our study extends this literature
by applying portfolio evaluation techniques specifically to funding rate arbitrage strategies.
Market efficiency and price discovery. The efficiency of cryptocurrency markets and
the speed of information incorporation remain active research questions. Ref. [14] test
market efficiency in Bitcoin using variance ratio tests, finding evidence of time-varying
efficiency with periods of both efficiency and inefficiency. Ref. [15] document what appear
to be substantial and persistent cross-exchange Bitcoin price deviations; they attribute these
to segmented capital flows, regulatory frictions, and sophisticated arbitrageur behavior,
though alternative explanations might also be plausible. Ref. [16] analyze price discovery
in Bitcoin spot and futures markets, finding that futures markets contribute significantly to
price formation. These findings motivate our investigation of funding rate integration across
exchanges and our tests of mean reversion and information flow using Granger causality.
Against this backdrop, our study investigates three fundamental questions. First, what
are the time-series properties of funding rate spreads across exchanges? Second, what are

Research Gap and Contribution
The existing literature exhibits two critical gaps that our study addresses. First,
empirical studies of funding rates focus primarily on single exchanges or aggregate data.
They neglect what may be substantial heterogeneity across trading venues. Ref. [2] analyze
aggregate Bitcoin perpetual futures data but do not examine cross-exchange variation
or institutional differences. Second, despite the rapid growth of decentralized perpetual
futures platforms, no study systematically compares CEX and DEX funding rate dynamics.
We address these gaps through the first high-frequency analysis of funding rates to
simultaneously encompass both centralized (11 CEX) and decentralized (15 DEX) cryp-
tocurrency exchanges. Our one-minute granularity panel dataset enables high-frequency
analysis of funding rate dynamics that no prior study has systematically examined across
both exchange types. The analysis enables us to: (1) characterize time-series properties of
funding rate spreads using stationarity tests, autocorrelation analysis, and GARCH volatil-
ity models; (2) quantify arbitrage opportunities and construct delta-neutral portfolios with
transaction cost adjustments; (3) examine information flow across exchanges via Granger
causality; and (4) assess market integration across exchange types.
3. Methodology
This section presents the framework for analyzing funding rate dynamics and con-
structing risk-managed arbitrage portfolios. The approach comprises five components:
data collection; time-series statistical analysis; cross-sectional market integration assess-
ment; arbitrage opportunity identification; and delta-neutral portfolio construction with
risk management.
3.1. Data
3.1.1. Data Sources and Collection
We collect funding rate data using APIs of the examined exchanges and a software
tool which aggregates and stores real-time perpetual futures data. Our dataset consists
of continuous one-minute granularity observations collected over eight consecutive days
(8–15 November 2025). This yields 35.7 million observations across 749 symbols with
multi-exchange coverage (minimum 2 exchanges per symbol). The high-frequency panel
structure enables both cross-sectional analysis of market fragmentation and time-series
analysis of funding rate dynamics, persistence, and convergence patterns.
For each exchange–symbol–timestamp observation, we record: (1) funding rate (in
basis points, bps, where 1 bps = 0.01%), (2) funding rate interval (1-h, 4-h, or 8-h), (3) Open
Interest (OI) rank as a liquidity proxy, and (4) timestamp with millisecond precision. Our
sample includes 11 centralized exchanges (BINANCE, BYBIT, OKX, KUCOIN, MEXC,
GATEIO, BITGET, BINGX, PHEMEX, CRYPTOCOM, HTX) and 15 decentralized exchanges
(HYPERLIQUID, DRIFT, PARADEX, KUMA, VEST, BLUEFIN, PACIFICA, LIGHTER,
EXTENDED, EDGEX, HIBACHI, ETHEREAL, VARIATIONAL, ASTER, WOOFI).
Data quality protocols: API rate limits required staggered requests with 100–200 ms
delays between calls to avoid throttling across exchanges. Missing data from brief exchange
downtimes totaled < 0.01% of expected observations (as reported in Table 1). We handle
missing observations via forward-filling for gaps ≤5 min, treating longer gaps as structural
NA where symbols are not offered. Some exchanges report funding rates only at discrete
intervals (1, 4, or 8 h) rather than continuously; we carry forward the most recent rate

3.1.3. Sample Filters
Two primary filters are applied: (1) Multi-exchange filter: We store data only for
symbols traded on ≥2 exchanges, reducing the sample from 1417 to 731 symbols with
arbitrage potential. (2) Major cryptocurrency subsample: For detailed time-series analysis,
we define major cryptocurrencies as the top 10 by Open Interest rank. OI rank directly mea-
sures perpetual futures market activity and remains more stable than market capitalization.
For portfolio analysis (Section 3.5), we focus on the top 20 arbitrage opportunities ranked
by spread magnitude rather than OI rank, as these represent the most economically signifi-
cant mispricings. (3) Outlier filter: We apply a conservative outlier threshold to remove
data quality errors. Funding rates exceeding ±5000 bps (equivalent to ±54,750% APY) are
flagged as likely API errors or flash crashes and excluded from analysis. This threshold is
set well beyond the 99.9th percentile of observed rates (maximum 2401 bps in our sample)
to retain legitimate extreme values while removing obvious anomalies. This filter removed
47 observations (0.0001% of the sample).

3.2.4. Information Flow and Granger Causality
To understand which exchanges might lead to changes in funding rates and how
information flows between CEX and DEX platforms, we estimate bivariate vector autore-
gression (VAR) models for strategically selected exchange pairs representing different
market structures [22]. Exchange e1 Granger-causes exchange e2 if past values of e1’s fund-
ing rate help predict e2’s current rate beyond what e2’s own history predicts. We select
optimal lag lengths using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [23] with a maximum
of 10 lags, balancing model fit against parameter proliferation. For most exchange pairs,
AIC selects 1–3 lags, reflecting rapid information transmission in high-frequency markets.
We test this using F-tests on the VAR coefficients and compute orthogonalized impulse
response functions to trace how shocks propagate across exchanges. We perform these
tests for five representative exchange pairs (BINANCE-HYPERLIQUID, BINANCE-BYBIT,
DRIFT-HYPERLIQUID, BINANCE-OKX, and KUCOIN-MEXC) to construct an information
flow network and specifically test directional causality between CEX and DEX platforms.
To address potential microstructure noise and timestamp misalignment at high frequen-
cies, we conduct robustness checks by resampling data to 5-min and 15-min intervals and
re-running Granger causality tests. Results remain qualitatively consistent across all three
frequencies: CEX platforms consistently Granger-cause DEX platforms, while the reverse is
rarely observed. This robustness suggests our main findings reflect genuine information
flow patterns rather than artifacts of 1-min sampling frequency.

3.5. Delta-Neutral Portfolio Construction and Risk Management
3.5.1. Portfolio Strategy and Transaction Costs
For each identified arbitrage opportunity (spread ≥20 bps), we construct a delta-
neutral portfolio by taking simultaneous long and short positions of equal notional value
across the two exchanges with the most extreme funding rates. We simulate portfolios with
equal position sizing of $10,000 per arbitrage opportunity.
Transaction costs: The costs charged at position entry and exit are critical for the net
profitability. We adjust gross profits for:
(1)
Taker fees (0.01–0.05% across exchanges in our sample, conservatively estimated at
0.05% in our simulations),
(2)
Slippage (conservatively estimated as 0.1% for liquid pairs with OI rank < 50, and 0.5%
for illiquid pairs), and
(3)
Gas costs for DEX transactions, which are negligible as most DEX in our sample
operate on low-cost blockchains (Solana, Arbitrum, Optimism) with costs < $0.01
per trade.
For our base case position size of $10,000 per side, transaction costs are:
•
Liquid markets (OI rank < 50): Entry costs of $30 (2 positions × $10,000 × 0.15%)
plus exit costs of $30, totaling $60 per round-trip
•
Illiquid markets (OI rank ≥50): Entry costs of $110 (2 positions × $10,000 × 0.55%)
plus exit costs of $110, totaling $220 per round-trip
Funding payments accrue continuously based on the instantaneous spread. While
funding rates are expressed as 8-h rates (the standard payment interval), we calculate
per-minute accrual by dividing the 8-h rate by 480 min, then sum across the holding period.
We construct a comprehensive fee table for all 26 exchanges based on published schedules
as of November 2025.
3.5.2. Spread Dynamics and Convergence Patterns
For each symbol exhibiting a significant spread, we track its evolution to understand
arbitrage sustainability. We examine whether spreads converge smoothly toward zero
or reverse suddenly (where exchanges swap relative positions). Spread reversals create
particular risk; arbitrageurs holding positions through a reversal experience losses as the
previously profitable spread turns negative.
For converging spreads, we measure: time for the spread to fall below the 20 bps
threshold, half-life (time to reach 50% of the initial spread from Equation (3)), and time to
near-complete convergence (10 bps). These metrics may inform optimal holding periods.
3.5.3. Forced Exit Protocol
Our portfolio simulations employ a conservative forced exit protocol: positions are
immediately closed when the funding rate spread turns negative (spread < 0 bps). This
threshold is chosen to strictly prevent losses from adverse spread reversals, where the
previously favorable funding differential begins working against the arbitrageur. While this
conservative approach limits downside risk, it may trigger premature exits when spreads
temporarily turn negative before recovering. Alternative exit thresholds (e.g., −5 bps or
−10 bps with tighter stop-loss rules) could potentially improve profitability by tolerating
brief negative excursions, but we adopt the zero-threshold approach to establish a baseline
risk-minimizing strategy. This methodological choice significantly affects the profitability
results reported in Section 4.6; readers should interpret the portfolio performance metrics
as conditional on this specific exit protocol rather than as general statements about funding
rate arbitrage profitability.

4.4. Time-Series Properties of Funding Rate Spreads
Stationarity: Only ETH exhibits what appears to be clear stationarity (ADF t = −7.20,
KPSS κ = 0.30, p = 0.10). Three symbols (BNB, ZEC, ASTER) appear non-stationary; six
show mixed evidence (Table 4).
Autocorrelation and persistence: Spreads exhibit extremely high persistence across
all symbols. First-order autocorrelations range from 0.966 to 0.998; current spreads strongly
predict next-period spreads. Even at lag 8, autocorrelations remain above 0.80 for all sym-
bols. Ljung-Box Q-tests overwhelmingly reject the null of no autocorrelation (p < 0.001),
with Q-statistics ranging from 39,970 to 51,736. This strong autocorrelation implies slow
adjustment to shocks and potential predictability, though it also means that large spreads
tend to persist for extended periods.
Mean reversion: ETH spreads revert rapidly with a half-life of approximately 20 min
(AR(1) coefficient 0.966, R2 = 0.93). This seems consistent with active arbitrage. Other
symbols show minimal or negligible mean reversion (AR(1) coefficients exceeding 0.96,
implying half-lives exceeding hours or even days). This heterogeneity may suggest varying
arbitrage efficiency; ETH appears to attract sophisticated arbitrageurs who rapidly eliminate
mispricings, while less liquid symbols exhibit considerably slower correction.
Volatility: GARCH(1,1) models for BTC, ETH, and SOL all exhibit what appears
to be integrated GARCH (IGARCH) with persistence α + β ≈1.0 (Table 5). BTC shows
α = 0.78, β = 0.22 (suggesting short-term shocks dominate); SOL displays α = 0.20,
β = 0.80 (where past volatility dominates); ETH falls somewhere in between (α = 0.93,
β = 0.07). IGARCH suggests that shocks to volatility may be permanent; conditional
variance follows what resembles a unit-root process, complicating risk management as
volatility does not revert to a long-run mean. Note that our 8-day observation window may
be insufficient to distinguish true IGARCH from near-integrated processes; this finding
should be interpreted cautiously.

4.7. Delta-Neutral Arbitrage Portfolio Performance
Delta-neutral portfolios for the top 20 arbitrage opportunities are simulated by simul-
taneously longing perpetuals on the lowest-rate exchange and shorting on the highest-rate
exchange ($10,000 per side). We assess net profitability after accounting for transaction
costs detailed in Section 3.5.
Spread reversal risk: We define “forced exit” as the point at which spreads turn
negative, requiring position closure to prevent accumulating losses (as the previously
profitable funding differential now works against the arbitrageur). When we classify
convergence patterns, 95% of portfolios (19 of 20) exhibit reversal patterns where exchanges
swap funding rate positions. Forced exits occurred for 12 of 20 portfolios when spreads
turned negative. The eight profitable portfolios (AIA, ASML, MERL, 0G, PARTI, KAVA,
GODS, LAYER) demonstrate that opportunities with sufficient spread magnitude and
duration can overcome transaction costs despite market volatility, though success is far
from guaranteed.
Profitability: Under our conservative forced exit protocol (spread < 0 bps), eight
portfolios generate positive net returns while twelve incur losses (Table 9). The best
performer AIA yields $1,433 profit over 2 days. Average net P&L across all 20 portfolios
is $22. This suggests that while transaction costs create a high profitability threshold,
opportunities with sufficient spread magnitude and duration can overcome these frictions,
though profitability is highly sensitive to the exit strategy employed.
Transaction cost burden: Transaction costs are charged at entry and exit only; for
illiquid markets (most top opportunities), this totals roughly $220 per round-trip. Funding
accrues per-minute (8-h rate divided by 480 min); this requires sustained spreads over
extended durations to overcome fixed costs. Consider that a 20 bps spread with this trade
size accrues just $0.042 per minute ($10,000 × 20 bps/10000/480), requiring 5263 min
(3.7 days) to generate enough funding to cover transaction costs. Opportunities with either
(1) high average spreads or (2) long durations appear capable of overcoming the threshold.
Risk-adjusted performance: Average Sharpe ratio is −7.40; even the best performing
portfolio (AIA) achieves a Sharpe of just −2.73. Average Sortino is −1.93. Maximum
drawdowns average −$117 (−1.17% of position size). The modest drawdowns may reflect
the protection offered by dynamic exit management when spreads turn negative, preventing
what could otherwise be more severe losses.

5. Discussion
Drawing on our analysis of funding rate observations across 26 cryptocurrency ex-
changes, we uncover what appears to be a two-tiered market structure. Centralized
exchanges appear to dominate price discovery with zero evidence of reverse information
flow from decentralized platforms. While roughly 17% of observations exhibit economically
significant arbitrage spreads, most top opportunities prove unprofitable under our conserva-
tive exit strategy after accounting for transaction costs and spread reversals. Cross-platform
market integration varies dramatically by exchange type; CEX-CEX correlations exceed
DEX-DEX correlations by 61%. These results may help reconcile the apparent paradox of
persistent large spreads with market efficiency. They suggest that visible mispricings reflect
equilibrium outcomes consistent with binding frictions (particularly transaction costs and
limited duration before spread reversals) rather than purely exploitable inefficiencies.

5.1. The Two-Tiered Market Structure
Our Granger causality analysis reveals what seems to be an unambiguous directional
pattern. Centralized exchanges lead funding rate innovations while decentralized platforms
operate as price takers. Among 14 CEX-DEX pairs tested, all significant relationships run
from CEX to DEX; none run the reverse direction. The 61% higher correlation among
CEX-CEX pairs (0.246) relative to DEX-DEX pairs (0.153) quantifies the integration gap
between exchange types. This may arise from DEX-specific frictions including blockchain
settlement delays, gas fee volatility, and fragmented liquidity across automated market
maker protocols. This asymmetry likely reflects three structural mechanisms that favor
CEX price leadership.
First, liquidity depth: Trading volume on perpetual futures concentrates overwhelm-
ingly on centralized platforms. During our observation period, the CEX exchanges account
for approximately 75–80% of total perpetual futures trading volume across major cryptocur-
rencies [27]. While the DEX-to-CEX perpetuals ratio has grown significantly throughout
2025 (rising from 3.7% to 11.7% over the year), CEX platforms still maintain dominant
market share. This liquidity concentration means that large traders and institutional arbi-
trageurs preferentially operate on CEX platforms where they can execute sizable positions
without material price impact. In contrast, DEX platforms operate with substantially lower
aggregate liquidity, limiting their ability to influence market-wide price discovery.
Second, execution speed: CEX platforms process transactions via centralized match-
ing engines with microsecond-level latency, enabling near-instantaneous price updates
when market conditions change. DEX platforms require blockchain transaction confirma-
tion, introducing settlement delays even on high-throughput chains. While most DEX
platforms in our sample operate on fast Layer-1 blockchains (Solana) or Layer-2/Layer-3
Ethereum solutions with sub-second to few-second settlement times, this still represents
orders of magnitude slower execution compared to CEX microsecond latency. This speed
differential means CEX rates adjust to new information before DEX platforms complete
transaction settlement, establishing CEX as the information source.
Third, cross-platform arbitrage infrastructure: Sophisticated arbitrageurs monitoring
funding rate differentials typically maintain their primary operations on CEX platforms
due to superior liquidity, lower operational friction, and faster execution. When these arbi-
trageurs observe CEX funding rate changes, they execute trades that propagate information
from CEX to DEX. However, the reverse flow is limited because few arbitrageurs monitor
DEX-originated information signals given DEX’s smaller market share and higher opera-
tional friction. This creates asymmetric information transmission favoring CEX leadership.
Together, these mechanisms create a tiered market structure where CEX platforms
function as price leaders while DEX platforms operate as price followers in the funding rate
discovery process. This pattern holds robustly across 1-min, 5-min, and 15-min sampling
frequencies (as documented in our robustness checks), confirming that CEX price leadership
reflects structural market differences.
This fragmentation somewhat mirrors the cross-exchange Bitcoin price deviations
documented by [15], who attribute persistent spreads to segmented capital flows, regulatory
frictions, and sophisticated arbitrageur behavior.
This integration gap translates directly into arbitrage opportunity sets. Most econom-
ically significant spreads (84%) involve CEX-DEX pairings, emerging precisely because
cross-type integration remains incomplete. Rather than being a secondary finding, this
pattern appears to define the fundamental structure of funding rate arbitrage markets; the
primary opportunity set exists at the boundary between centralized and decentralized
platforms, not within either ecosystem. For practitioners, this suggests that successful

5.2. Arbitrage Opportunities: Reconciling Perception with Reality
The finding that most top arbitrage opportunities generate negative returns under our
conservative exit protocol challenges the “free money” narrative pervading popular discus-
sions of cryptocurrency funding rate arbitrage. While our analysis identifies 596 symbols
with average spreads exceeding 20 basis points (superficially suggesting widespread profit
potential), portfolio simulations reveal that transaction costs and spread reversals systemat-
ically prevent profitability for most opportunities when positions are exited immediately
upon spread reversal.
This apparent contradiction between opportunity prevalence (17% of observations)
and limited realized profitability may arise because visible spreads represent equilibrium
mispricings consistent with binding frictions. These findings align with [3]’s theoretical
prediction that trading costs create pricing bounds within which mispricings can persist
in equilibrium. Markets appear more efficient than gross spreads imply; the persistence
of 20–50 bps differentials does not necessarily indicate market failure but rather may
reflect that these spreads generate insufficient funding accrual to overcome transaction
cost thresholds before spreads reverse. Only opportunities with exceptional characteristics
(combining high average spreads with sufficient duration) generate cumulative returns
exceeding transaction cost thresholds.
We note that the observed IGARCH behavior may partly reflect our sampling structure,
where 8-h funding rates are observed every minute, rather than pure economic persistence.
Distinguishing true unit-root processes from near-integrated dynamics typically requires
months or years of data. Given our 8-day sample window, we interpret these results as
evidence of strong short-term persistence rather than permanent shocks.
For practitioners during our observation period, our results might provide a decision
framework emphasizing that both high spreads and sufficient duration matter. Consider
the comparison between AIA and INTC. AIA’s 732.5 bps average spread maintained for
2 days before forced exit generates $0.57 per minute in funding accrual, accumulating to
$1653 in gross payments over the holding period; after transaction costs, net profit reaches
$1433 (representing 12,953% APY). In stark contrast, INTC’s 6.57 bps average spread over
6 days 6 h generates only $180 in gross funding, insufficient to overcome transaction cost
thresholds, resulting in a $40 loss. This demonstrates that neither high spreads alone nor
long duration alone guarantee profitability; both factors must align. Successful arbitrage
appears to require identifying opportunities with sufficiently high spreads combined with
adequate duration before inevitable spread reversals force exit.
Dynamic risk management is imperative. The prevalence of spread reversals (affecting
95% of opportunities) highlights a critical operational requirement; passive delta-neutral
positions become loss-makers when spreads turn negative. Profitable arbitrage seems to
demand immediate exit protocols triggered when spreads reverse to negative territory.
Rather than passive “set-and-forget” positions, practitioners must implement active mon-
itoring featuring: (1) real-time spread tracking to detect when spreads approach zero,
(2) immediate position unwinding when spreads turn negative to prevent accumulating
losses, and (3) realistic profit expectations. The contrast between profitable (AIA, KAVA,
MERL, ASML) and unprofitable portfolios reveals that success requires not just identifying
high spreads initially, but actively exiting when spreads turn negative via disciplined
exit execution.
From an academic perspective, these findings may help reconcile the prior literature
documenting large cryptocurrency arbitrage spreads [15] with market efficiency theory.

6. Conclusions
This study offers the first systematic cross-platform examination of cryptocurrency
funding rates, analyzing observations across centralized and decentralized exchanges
over eight days. The findings suggest a two-tiered market structure where centralized ex-
changes appear to dominate price discovery while decentralized platforms operate largely
as price takers (CEX-CEX correlations exceed DEX-DEX correlations by 61%). This struc-
tural divide creates what we observe as the primary arbitrage setting; most economically
significant funding rate spreads emerge at the CEX-DEX boundary. These spreads may
reflect incomplete cross-platform integration rather than purely exploitable inefficiencies.
The paradox of large and widespread spreads coexisting with limited profitability
might be resolved through equilibrium mispricing under binding frictions. Though roughly
17% of observations exhibit high spreads (exceeding 20 basis points), profitability under
conservative exit protocols proves elusive for most opportunities after transaction costs
and spread reversals. Transaction costs combined with our zero-tolerance exit threshold
create profitability barriers that filter most opportunities, though alternative exit strategies
may yield different results.
For practitioners during our observation period, these findings challenge the “free
money” narrative while potentially offering direction. Successful funding rate arbitrage
appears to require several elements: high spreads combined with sufficient duration to
overcome transaction cost thresholds; focus on CEX-DEX opportunities where structural
integration gaps persist; immediate exit protocols when spreads turn negative to prevent
losses. Realistic profit expectations matter. The contrast between profitable and unprofitable
strategies demonstrates something important; passive delta-neutral positions tend to fail.
Success appears to require active exit management when spreads turn negative.
From an academic perspective, our findings provide evidence supporting the limits-to-
arbitrage framework in cryptocurrency derivatives markets. Observable mispricings do not
necessarily signify market inefficiency; they might represent equilibrium pricing among
heterogeneous frictions. The effective integration within CEX environments, but frag-
mented cross-platform arbitrage between CEX and DEX, improves our understanding
of how market design influences price formation in hybrid centralized-decentralized fi-
nancial systems. The absence of evidence for DEX-to-CEX information flow suggests
that centralized platforms retain control over the microstructure of the derivative market,
even as decentralized finance expands. This could influence systemic risk assessment and
regulatory oversight approaches.
Future research should extend our eight-day observation window across bull, bear,
and high-volatility setups. Identifying whether the two-tiered structure persists under
varying market conditions would clarify whether it represents a fundamental feature or a
context-specific phenomenon. Developing predictive models to forecast spread reversals
(using machine learning and order flow imbalances) can potentially transform passive
arbitrage into dynamic, risk-managed strategies. As the boundary between centralized
and decentralized finance continues to evolve, understanding the frictions that sustain
persistent mispricings remains essential. In addition, the analysis in this study employs
a conservative forced exit threshold (spread < 0 bps), which limits losses but may exit
prematurely when spreads temporarily turn negative before recovering. Sensitivity analysis

position closure.
